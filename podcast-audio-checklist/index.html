A practical podcast audio editing checklist for weekly shows

Use this checklist before you send an episode to an audio editor or publish a finished file. It focuses on audio-only interview shows, where consistent recordings and a clear handoff matter more than complicated production tricks.

1. Get the recordings right

  • Record every speaker on a separate track where possible. Mark who is speaking on each track, and preserve the raw originals.
  • Wear headphones to reduce speaker audio leaking into the microphone. Leave a few seconds of room tone.
  • Keep microphone distance steady; avoid letting the recording clip. If a sample distorts badly, re-recording is usually safer than relying on restoration.
  • Save the export with unambiguous filenames such as show_042_host.wav and show_042_guest.wav. Do not change sample rate merely to match a desired output name.

2. Prepare an editor brief

  • Include target release date, approximate raw runtime, speaker count, number of separate tracks and file sample format.
  • List mistakes or cuts using timestamps. Distinguish essential editorial cuts from optional polish.
  • Supply intro/outro/music you have permission to use, with instructions for fade-ins, fades and placement.
  • Provide a link to a recent episode as a sound reference, but do not ask an editor to imitate another creator's protected content.

3. Edit the dialogue

  • Listen to every channel before processing. Reduce hum and consistent hiss gently; over-processing can make voices brittle.
  • Match dialogue levels across speakers before adding aggressive compression. Clean problematic plosives, breaths and clicks only where distracting.
  • Preserve the natural meaning of the conversation. A short silence can be intentional; cutting every pause often sounds unnatural.
  • Check edits with headphones and speakers for abrupt transitions, strange background changes and clipped words.

4. Prepare the final exports

  • Agree the final loudness and true-peak specification with the publisher or hosting service. A single generic number is not a guarantee for every distributor.
  • Export a finished listening copy in the requested format (commonly MP3) and a lossless WAV archive if agreed.
  • Play the opening, several transitions and the ending in the exported file; check mono compatibility, clipping, gaps and metadata.
  • Keep the original project and raw files private, and confirm the agreed retention/deletion policy before exchanging personal content.

5. Make recurring delivery easy

  • Use the same filename convention, release checklist, sound reference and cut-notes format each week.
  • Track actual editing time per episode, including revision time. Keep a simple record of recurring problems you can fix while recording.
  • Ask for one combined revision note rather than multiple fragmented messages.
